The design process

Before hiring an interior designer: questions for the first meeting

You do not need every answer before speaking with an interior designer. Bring a clear starting point and use the conversation to understand scope, process and responsibilities.

By DMS StudioPublished 2026-10-09Updated 2026-10-15

Editorial photograph · Unsplash. Not a DMS project.

Quick answer

Before your first interior design meeting, gather the property location, available plan, current photos, room priorities, working budget and timing. Ask what the proposed scope includes, what happens next and how changes are documented.

Bring the essentials

Share the property location and type, a floor plan if available, and photographs of the current rooms. Note whether the home is occupied, newly handed over, under construction or being renovated. List the rooms you want to change and the ones that already work. This helps a designer understand the context before suggesting a direction. If measurements are approximate, say so; an early plan should not be mistaken for a site-verified drawing.

Describe the outcome you need

A useful brief explains what should improve, not only what style you like. Perhaps the entry needs storage, the kitchen needs a clearer work sequence, or a salon needs a more comfortable waiting area. Share the people who use the space, equipment or furniture that must remain, and any known building rules. Prioritise the changes as essential, preferred or optional. That makes trade-offs easier to discuss if the scope needs to fit a budget or programme.

Ask for a written scope

Clarify whether the proposal covers design, drawings, material selection, procurement, contractor coordination, site visits, installation or styling. Ask what the studio supplies and what is handled by you or a separate specialist. Confirm how professional fees, taxes, delivery, appliances, site preparation and changes are treated. A headline price is difficult to compare without the same scope and assumptions. Request a written outline before committing so both sides can refer to the same expectations.

Discuss decisions, approvals and timing

Ask how concepts are presented, who approves selections and how revisions are recorded. Discuss the sequence of design decisions and what information is needed before work begins. A start or completion date depends on the agreed scope, site readiness, approvals, material availability and other project conditions. A responsible conversation identifies these dependencies instead of promising a fixed timeline before the details are known.

Agree on a practical next step

At the end of the meeting, ask whether the next action is a site visit, measured survey, proposal, concept package or another discussion. Find out what you should provide and when the studio will respond. If you are comparing multiple providers, use the same property information and ask each one to describe inclusions and exclusions clearly. You can then compare the approach, scope and communication rather than relying on a presentation alone.

A commercial example: Essentia Salon

Essentia Salon in New Chandigarh is a DMS commercial interior project. Its project page documents the salon’s storefront and interior spaces. For a salon brief, questions about client arrival, waiting, treatment stations, staff movement, product display, storage and cleaning can help shape the planning conversation. The actual layout and services for another salon would still need a site review and an agreed scope; one project is an example, not a ready-made specification.

How can I compare interior design proposals fairly?

Compare proposals against the same rooms, drawings and project assumptions. Check design deliverables, materials, quantities, procurement, installation, specialist work, taxes, delivery, site visits and change handling. Ask for exclusions and responsibilities in writing. This creates a clearer comparison than looking at a single total without understanding what it covers.

Useful answers

Common questions

Should I have a fixed budget before the first meeting?+

A working range is helpful, even if it may change. Discuss priorities and scope openly, then ask for a written proposal that explains its assumptions and exclusions.

Can I ask an interior designer for a guaranteed completion date?+

Ask how the programme is developed and what it depends on. Dates should be discussed against scope, site readiness, approvals and supply conditions rather than promised without those details.

What documents should I bring?+

Bring a plan if available, current photographs, the property location, a list of priorities, known building requirements and any material or furniture references you want to discuss.
